Manufacturing units, service providers, traders, and infrastructure contractors operating as Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs) in Uttar Pradesh navigate two parallel legal imperatives: maintaining error-free compliance with the **Central Goods and Services Tax (CGST) Act, 2017** and the **Uttar Pradesh Goods and Services Tax (UPGST) Act, 2017**, while enforcing timely invoice collections against corporate and government buyers under the **Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises Development (MSMED) Act, 2006**.

Across industrial clusters across India, Kanpur, Noida, Ghaziabad, Varanasi, and Moradabad, automated GST portal scrutiny (Form GST ASMT-10) and mismatches between GSTR-2B and GSTR-3B routinely trigger Input Tax Credit (ITC) demand notices and bank account attachments under Section 83/79. Simultaneously, working-capital bottlenecks caused by delayed buyer payments require strategic invocation of the **MSME Samadhaan** portal and the **Zonal Micro and Small Enterprises Facilitation Councils (MSEFC)** in Uttar Pradesh.

## What Are the Strict Conditions for Claiming Input Tax Credit (ITC) Under Section 16 of the GST Act?

Under Section 16(2) of the CGST and UPGST Acts, 2017, a registered taxpayer is entitled to avail Input Tax Credit only when six cumulative statutory conditions are met, including actual receipt of goods or services, auto-population of the invoice in Form GSTR-2B, and actual payment of tax by the supplier to the government.

Historically, taxpayers relied on physical tax invoices and E-Way bills to defend Input Tax Credit claims. However, following progressive statutory amendments—specifically **Section 16(2)(aa)** and **Section 16(2)(ba)** read with **Rule 36(4) of the CGST Rules**—automated system matching governs ITC eligibility:

| Statutory Clause          | Mandatory Compliance Condition for Buyer                                                                                    | Operational Risk & Mitigation                                                                                                                                                  |
| :------------------------ | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Section 16(2)(a)**      | Possession of a valid Tax Invoice or Debit Note issued under Section 31.                                                    | Ensure vendor GSTIN, HSN/SAC code, and place of supply are verified before booking.                                                                                            |
| **Section 16(2)(aa)**     | Details of the invoice/debit note have been furnished by the supplier in **Form GSTR-1** and communicated to the recipient. | Do not avail manual ITC outside GSTR-2B; hold back the GST component of vendor payment until GSTR-1 filing is verified.                                                        |
| **Section 16(2)(b)**      | The recipient has **actually received** the goods or services.                                                              | Preserve physical Delivery Challans, E-Way Bills, Lorry Receipts (Bilty), weighbridge slips, and gate-entry registers to defeat "fake invoicing/circular trading" allegations. |
| **Section 16(2)(ba)**     | ITC in respect of the supply is not restricted in **Form GSTR-2B** under Section 38.                                        | Reconcile purchase registers against GSTR-2B monthly before filing GSTR-3B on the 20th of the succeeding month.                                                                |
| **Section 16(2)(c)**      | Tax charged in respect of such supply has been **actually paid to the Government** by the supplier.                         | Incorporate an **ITC Indemnity & Set-Off Clause** in all Vendor Purchase Orders allowing deduction of unpaid GST + 18% interest from subsequent vendor bills.                  |
| **Section 16(2) Proviso** | Buyer must pay the supplier the value of supply + tax within **180 days** from the date of invoice.                         | Under Rule 37, failure to pay the vendor within 180 days requires reversal of ITC along with interest under Section 50 (re-claimable upon actual payment).                     |

## How Should Taxpayers Respond to GST Scrutiny Notices (ASMT-10) and Show Cause Notices (Sections 73, 74 & 74A)?

When the State Tax (UPGST) or Central Tax officer detects discrepancies in returns, proceedings typically begin with a Scrutiny Notice in Form GST ASMT-10 under Section 61, escalating—if unresolved—to an Intimation/Show Cause Notice in Form GST DRC-01 under Section 73, Section 74, or Section 74A.

Ignoring portal notices uploaded under the _"Additional Notices and Orders"_ tab on the GST portal frequently leads to _ex-parte_ adjudication orders in **Form GST DRC-07**, followed by summary recovery and bank account attachment after 90 days.

### Procedural Strategy for Defending GST Adjudication & Appeals in Uttar Pradesh

1. **Distinguishing Section 73 vs. Section 74 (and Section 74A from FY 2024-25):**
   - **Section 73 (Non-Fraud / Bona Fide Interpretation or Reconciliation Discrepancies):** Penalty is capped at **10% of the tax amount or Rs. 10,000, whichever is higher** (and zero penalty if tax and Section 50 interest are paid before issuance of the Show Cause Notice or within 30 days thereof).
   - **Section 74 (Fraud, Willful Misstatement, or Suppression of Facts to Evade Tax):** Attracts penalty up to **100% of the tax demand** and extended limitation periods. Field officers in Uttar Pradesh frequently invoke Section 74 mechanically even for simple GSTR-3B vs. GSTR-2A/2B or E-Way Bill typographical mismatches. Following authoritative decisions of the **Allahabad High Court**, a notice under Section 74 is unsustainable unless the department specifically pleads and proves conscious _mens rea_ (intent to evade tax).
   - **Section 74A (Applicable from FY 2024-25 onwards):** Enacted by the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024, Section 74A unifies the limitation period for issuing notices (42 months from the due date of the annual return) while maintaining separate penalty slabs for non-fraud vs. fraud cases and extending the window for reduced/nil penalty settlement from 30 days to **60 days**.
2. **Mandatory Right of Personal Hearing under Section 75(4):** Under Section 75(4) of the CGST/UPGST Act, the adjudicating officer **must** grant an opportunity of personal hearing whenever a written request is made or whenever an adverse decision is contemplated. Orders passed by UPGST officers across India or other UP circles without granting a personal hearing—or by fixing the personal hearing date _on or before_ the last date for filing the written reply—are routinely set aside on writ jurisdiction under **Article 226 before the Allahabad High Court (India Bench and Prayagraj)** for violation of natural justice.
3. **First Appeal under Section 107 (Form GST APL-01):** An appeal against an adjudication order (DRC-07) lies before the **Additional Commissioner (Appeals) / Joint Commissioner (Appeals)** within **3 months** of communication of the order (extendable by 1 month), requiring a mandatory pre-deposit of **10% of the disputed tax amount** (reduced from earlier higher ceilings by the 2024 amendment), which automatically stays recovery of the balance demand under Section 107(11)/(7).

## How Does Payment Recovery Work Under the MSMED Act, 2006 (MSME Samadhaan)?

Under Sections 15 to 18 of the MSMED Act, 2006, any Udyam-registered Micro or Small Enterprise that supplies goods or renders services is statutorily entitled to receive payment within a maximum of 45 days from the day of acceptance, failing which the buyer must pay compound interest at three times the RBI Bank Rate.

For Micro and Small Enterprises in Uttar Pradesh facing withheld bills from private corporations, EPC main contractors, or government departments, the **MSME Samadhaan** mechanism before the **Uttar Pradesh Micro and Small Enterprises Facilitation Council (MSEFC)** offers a decisive statutory edge over ordinary civil suits:

### Critical Legal Rules Governing MSME Samadhaan Claims

- **Mandatory Udyam Registration _Prior_ to the Underlying Contract:** Following the Supreme Court's landmark rulings in **_Silpi Industries v. Kerala State Road Transport Corporation_ (2021)** and **_Vaishno Enterprises v. Hamilton Medical AG_ (2022)**, to invoke the jurisdiction of the MSEFC under Section 18, the supplier **must have held a valid Udyam (or erstwhile Udyog Aadhaar) registration as a Micro or Small Enterprise on the date the contract was entered into and the goods/services were supplied**. Obtaining Udyam registration _after_ the supply purely to file a recovery claim is legally barred.
- **45-Day Maximum Payment Ceiling (Section 15) & 3x Compound Interest (Section 16):**
  - If there is no written credit period agreed between the supplier and buyer, payment is due within **15 days** of delivery/deemed acceptance.
  - Even if a written Purchase Order stipulates a 90-day or 120-day credit period, **Section 15 overrides the contract and caps the maximum permissible credit period at 45 days**.
  - From the 46th day onward, **Section 16** mandates compound interest with **monthly rests at 3 times the Bank Rate notified by the Reserve Bank of India**, notwithstanding anything contained in any contract to the contrary.
- **Two-Stage Conciliation and Arbitration Before UP MSEFC (Section 18):**
  1. The supplier files a delayed payment application on the **MSME Samadhaan portal**, which is convertible into a formal reference before the **Zonal MSEFC in Uttar Pradesh** (e.g., India / Kanpur zones).
  2. Under Section 18(2), the Council first initiates **mandatory conciliation** (applying Sections 65 to 81 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996).
  3. If conciliation fails without settlement, the Council under **Section 18(3)** takes up the dispute for **arbitration** itself (or refers it to an arbitration centre), culminating in a binding Arbitral Award executable as a civil court decree—which the buyer cannot challenge under Section 34 without depositing **75% of the awarded amount** in court under Section 19.
