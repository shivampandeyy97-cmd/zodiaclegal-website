When negotiating high-value infrastructure, EPC, manufacturing, distribution, or corporate agreements—or when an active breach escalates into a legal dispute—in-house counsel and promoters face a threshold strategic question: **Does commercial arbitration under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 deliver faster, more predictable recovery than civil litigation before specialized Commercial Courts under the Commercial Courts Act, 2015?**

Over the past decade, Parliament has substantially modernized both tracks. The **2015, 2019, and 2021 Amendments to the Arbitration Act** imposed strict Section 29A statutory clocks and narrowed judicial interference, while the **Commercial Courts Act, 2015** introduced hard forfeiture of the right to file a Written Statement after 120 days, Case Management Hearings (Order XV-A CPC), and Summary Judgments (Order XIII-A CPC). In Uttar Pradesh, dedicated **Commercial Courts across India**, Kanpur, Gautam Buddha Nagar, and other districts operate alongside the supervisory arbitration jurisdiction of the **Allahabad High Court (India Bench and Principal Seat)**.

## How Do Arbitration and Commercial Court Litigation Compare Across Key Legal Parameters?

Arbitration is a consensual, private adjudication mechanism created by a valid written arbitration agreement under Section 7 of the Arbitration Act, 1996, whereas Commercial Court litigation is a statutory judicial proceeding governed by the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 and the amended Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 for disputes of Rs. 3,00,000 or more.

| Parameter                 | Commercial Arbitration (1996 Act)                                                                                                  | Commercial Court Litigation (2015 Act)                                                                                                   |
| :------------------------ | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Jurisdictional Basis**  | Requires a written Arbitration Agreement under **Section 7** (contract clause or separate submission agreement).                   | Statutory jurisdiction over any "Commercial Dispute" under **Section 2(1)(c)** valued at **Rs. 3 Lakh+**.                                |
| **Pre-Institution Step**  | Invocation Notice under **Section 21** (mandatory to commence arbitration and stop limitation).                                    | Mandatory Pre-Institution Mediation under **Section 12A** before DLSA (unless urgent interim relief is sought).                          |
| **Adjudicator Selection** | Parties nominate arbitrators with domain expertise (e.g., retired Judges, Chartered Engineers, Senior Counsel).                    | Adjudicated by a designated District Judge-level Commercial Court Judge.                                                                 |
| **Statutory Time Limits** | Pleadings within **6 months** (Sec 23(4)); Award within **12 months** of pleadings completion (**Sec 29A**), +6 months by consent. | Written Statement strictly within **30 to 120 days** (forfeited thereafter); arguments within 6 months of first Case Management Hearing. |
| **Confidentiality**       | Strictly confidential under **Section 42A** (except where disclosure is required for enforcement).                                 | Public court record and open-court proceedings.                                                                                          |
| **Finality & Appeal**     | No review on merits; only narrow **Section 34** set-aside petition and limited **Section 37** appeal.                              | First Appeal on law and facts to the **Commercial Appellate Division** of the High Court within 60 days (**Section 13**).                |
| **Cost Structure**        | Parties pay Arbitral Tribunal fees (often guided by the **Fourth Schedule**) and venue costs, recoverable under Section 31A.       | Statutory ad-valorem Court Fees under the Court Fees Act (as applicable in UP) plus counsel fees.                                        |

## How Does the Statutory Timeline Work in Arbitration Under Sections 21, 11, and 29A?

Under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, arbitral proceedings formally commence on the date the respondent receives a written notice invoking arbitration under Section 21. Once the tribunal is constituted and pleadings are completed within six months under Section 23(4), Section 29A mandates an award within 12 months.

To ensure an arbitration seated across India moves swiftly without procedural derailment, practitioners follow a disciplined statutory sequence:

1. **Section 21 Notice of Invocation:** A defective dispute notice that merely demands money without explicitly invoking the arbitration clause and proposing/nominating an arbitrator in terms of the contract does **not** satisfy Section 21 (_Alupro Building Systems Pvt. Ltd. v. Ozone Overseas Pvt. Ltd._). Because Section 21 stops the running of the 3-year limitation clock under the Limitation Act, 1963, precision at this stage is critical.
2. **Elimination of Unilateral Appointments (Section 12(5) & Seventh Schedule):** Many legacy contracts with PSUs, NBFCs, or large corporations contain clauses permitting one party's Managing Director to unilaterally appoint a Sole Arbitrator. Following **_Perkins Eastman_ (2019)** and the five-judge Constitution Bench decision in **_CORE v. ECI SPIC SMO MCML (JV)_ (2024)**, unilateral appointment of a sole arbitrator—or forcing a counterparty to choose from a curated, non-broad-based panel controlled by one party—is invalid unless expressly waived in writing _after_ the dispute has arisen.
3. **Section 11(6) Appointment by the High Court:** If the counterparty fails to agree on the Sole Arbitrator or fails to nominate its wing arbitrator within **30 days** of receiving the Section 21 notice, the claimant files an arbitration petition under Section 11(6) before the **Allahabad High Court (India Bench for central UP districts)**. At the Section 11 stage, the High Court confines its examination strictly to the _prima facie_ existence of the arbitration agreement under Section 11(6A), leaving arbitrability and limitation merits to the arbitral tribunal under **Section 16 (_Kompetenz-Kompetenz_)**.

## How Do Urgent Interim Reliefs Operate Under Section 9 and Section 17 vs. Commercial Courts?

Before an arbitral tribunal is constituted, a party can approach the jurisdictional Commercial Court under Section 9 of the Arbitration Act for urgent interim measures—such as attachment before judgment, bank account security, or preservation of subject goods. Once the tribunal is constituted, Section 17 becomes the primary forum.

In high-stakes commercial disputes across India—such as wrongful termination of a concession agreement, diversion of joint-venture revenues, or threatened disposal of unencumbered assets by a defaulting debtor—waiting 12 months for a final decree or award can render recovery illusory.

- **Section 9 Petitions Before the Commercial Court:** Even before serving a Section 21 notice (provided arbitral proceedings are commenced within **90 days** of the interim order under Section 9(2)), the Commercial Court in India can grant:
  - Interim injunctions restraining alienation of immovable properties or termination of determinable contracts where legally permissible;
  - Direction to the respondent to furnish **bank guarantees or security deposits** covering the amount in dispute (applying Order XXXVIII Rule 5 CPC principles in line with _Essar House Pvt. Ltd. v. Arcellor Mittal Nippon Steel India Ltd._ (2022));
  - Appointment of a Court Receiver or Local Commissioner to inspect and inventory plant, machinery, or stock.
- **Urgent Interim Relief Exemption Under Section 12A (Commercial Suits):** Where no arbitration clause exists and the plaintiff files a direct suit before the Commercial Court, seeking genuine urgent interim relief (Order XXXIX Rules 1 & 2 CPC) allows the plaintiff to bypass the mandatory pre-institution mediation window under **Section 12A of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015**, subject to the Commercial Court verifying that the urgency is _bona fide_ and not a device to circumvent mediation (_Yamini Manohar v. T.K.D. Keerthi_, 2023).

## How Are Arbitral Awards Challenged (Section 34) and Enforced (Section 36) in Uttar Pradesh?

Under Section 36 of the Arbitration Act, once the 90-day period to file a Section 34 challenge expires (or if a Section 34 petition is filed without obtaining an express stay order), the arbitral award is enforced directly by the Commercial Court in the same manner as a decree of a civil court under Order XXI of the CPC.

### Narrow Grounds Under Section 34 (No Merits Review)

After the 2015 Amendment, a Commercial Court hearing a **Section 34 petition** cannot sit as a court of appeal to re-appreciate evidence or substitute its own interpretation of contract clauses for a plausible view taken by the arbitrator (_Ssangyong Engineering & Construction Co. Ltd. v. NHAI_, 2019). For domestic awards, intervention is restricted to:

- Incapacity of a party, invalid arbitration agreement, or lack of proper notice of appointment/proceedings;
- The award dealing with a dispute outside the scope of submission to arbitration;
- Conflict with the **fundamental policy of Indian law** or basic notions of morality or justice; or
- **Patent illegality** appearing on the face of the award (Section 34(2A)), provided that an award shall not be set aside merely on the ground of an erroneous application of the law or by re-appreciation of evidence.

Furthermore, under **Section 36(2) and (3)**, merely filing a Section 34 challenge in the Commercial Court in India does **not** operate as an automatic stay on enforcement of the money award; to obtain a stay, the judgment-debtor is ordinarily required to deposit up to **100% of the awarded sum (in cash or bank guarantee)** in court.
