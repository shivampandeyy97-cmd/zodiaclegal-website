For consumer brands, pharmaceutical companies, software platforms, educational institutions, and manufacturing enterprises in Uttar Pradesh, securing statutory trademark registration under the **Trade Marks Act, 1999** and the **Trade Marks Rules, 2017** is foundational to protecting brand equity. While Indian common law recognizes the tort of "passing off" for unregistered marks under Section 27(2), only a registered proprietor enjoys the exclusive statutory right under **Section 28** to sue for **trademark infringement** under **Section 134** before the District/Commercial Court where the plaintiff actually and voluntarily resides or carries on business.

Because over 70% of trademark applications in India encounter an automated or examiner-issued **Examination Report** citing objections under **Section 9 (Absolute Grounds)** or **Section 11 (Relative Grounds)**, strategic pre-filing clearance, accurate Nice Class specification, and rigorous prosecution are essential.

## How Do You Select the Correct Nice Classification and Filing Format Before Submitting Form TM-A?

India follows the 11th/12th Edition of the International (Nice) Classification of Goods and Services, which divides all commercial activities into 45 classes (Classes 1 to 34 for Goods and Classes 35 to 45 for Services). Applicants can file a single-class application or a multi-class application (Series/Class 99) using Form TM-A.

Before filing Form TM-A with the **Trade Marks Registry (New Delhi branch for Uttar Pradesh applicants)**, counsel must conduct a two-tier clearance search on the IP India Public Search database:

1. **Identical & Phonetic Search:** Searching both exact word strings and phonetic equivalents in the target class and allied cross-classes (e.g., searching both Class 5 for pharmaceuticals and Class 35 for medical retail, or Class 9 for downloadable software apps and Class 42 for SaaS/cloud software development).
2. **Vienna Code Search (for Logos/Devices):** If the mark contains a graphical symbol, emblem, or geometric figure, a search under the **International Classification of the Figurative Elements of Marks (Vienna Classification)** is mandatory.

### "Proposed to Be Used" vs. "Prior User" Claim (Rule 25)

An application can be filed either on a **"Proposed to be Used"** basis (before commercial launch) or by claiming a **Prior User Date** (the exact day, month, and year the brand was first used commercially in India). Under **Rule 25 of the Trade Marks Rules, 2017**, any application claiming prior use **must** be accompanied by a notarized/e-stamped **User Affidavit** annexing continuous documentary evidence from the claimed date—such as GST tax invoices, packaging proofs, audited advertisement expenditure certificates, domain WHOIS records, and FSSAI/drug licenses. Claiming a backdated user date without attaching a compliant Rule 25 User Affidavit automatically triggers a formal examination deficiency.

## How Do You Overcome Section 9 and Section 11 Objections in the Examination Report?

Within 1 to 3 months of filing Form TM-A, the Examiner of Trade Marks issues an Examination Report. If the mark is marked "Objected" under Section 9 (lack of distinctiveness/descriptiveness) or Section 11 (conflict with earlier marks), the applicant must file a written legal reply within 30 days of receipt.

Failing to respond within the **30-day statutory deadline** results in the application being treated as **"Abandoned"** under Section 132 read with Rule 33(4).

### 1. Answering Section 9(1)(a) & 9(1)(b) Objections (Absolute Grounds)

Section 9 bars the registration of marks that are devoid of distinctive character or consist exclusively of indications designating the kind, quality, quantity, intended purpose, value, or geographical origin of the goods or services.

- **Legal Strategy:**
  - Demonstrate that the mark is **coined, arbitrary, or at most suggestive** rather than directly descriptive of the underlying goods (applying the classic spectrum of distinctiveness).
  - Invoke the **Proviso to Section 9(1)** by proving **acquired distinctiveness (secondary meaning)** through extensive, uninterrupted prior commercial use, sales turnover, and consumer association evidenced by the Rule 25 User Affidavit.
  - Rely on the principle of **anti-dissection under Section 17**, establishing that a composite mark must be evaluated as an integrated whole rather than dissected into isolated dictionary components.

### 2. Answering Section 11(1) Objections (Relative Grounds)

Section 11 bars registration where a mark is identical or deceptively similar to an earlier registered or pending trademark in respect of identical or similar goods/services, creating a likelihood of confusion.

- **Legal Strategy:**
  - Apply the Supreme Court's tests from **_Cadila Health Care Ltd. v. Cadila Pharmaceuticals Ltd._ (2001)** and **_Kaviraj Pandit Durga Dutt Sharma v. Navratna Pharmaceutical Laboratories_ (1965)** to distinguish the applied mark from each cited conflicting mark in the Examination Report.
  - Establish **visual, phonetic, and structural dissimilarity** when compared side-by-side as wholes.
  - Differentiate the **specification of goods/services, trade channels, and class of purchasers** (e.g., sophisticated B2B institutional buyers vs. casual retail consumers), or establish **honest and concurrent use under Section 12**.

If the Examiner is not satisfied by the written reply alone, the Registry schedules a **Show Cause Hearing** (conducted via video-conferencing before the Hearing Officer at the New Delhi Registry), where the authorized Trademark Attorney or Advocate argues the precedent and evidentiary record orally.

## What Happens During the 4-Month Journal Publication and Opposition Stage (Section 21)?

Once the Examiner or Hearing Officer accepts the trademark application, it is advertised in the official weekly Trade Marks Journal. Under Section 21 of the Trade Marks Act, 1999, any person may file a Notice of Opposition (Form TM-O) within four months from the date the Journal is made available to the public.

If no opposition is filed within the four-month statutory window, the application proceeds automatically to registration, and the digital **Certificate of Registration under Section 23(2)** is generated. However, if a third party files Form TM-O, a quasi-judicial opposition proceeding commences:

| Opposition Stage                                 | Statutory Rule (2017 Rules) | Strict Deadline                                        | Consequence of Default                                                                                      |
| :----------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1. Notice of Opposition (Form TM-O)**          | Section 21(1) & Rule 42     | Within **4 months** of Journal publication             | Unextendable; late opposition cannot be entertained.                                                        |
| **2. Applicant's Counter-Statement**             | Section 21(2) & Rule 44     | Within **2 months** of service of Notice of Opposition | Application is deemed **Abandoned** if Counter-Statement is not filed in 2 months.                          |
| **3. Opponent's Evidence in Support (Rule 45)**  | Rule 45                     | Within **2 months** of service of Counter-Statement    | Opposition is deemed **Abandoned** under Rule 45(2) if Opponent neither files affidavit nor relies on TM-O. |
| **4. Applicant's Evidence in Support (Rule 46)** | Rule 46                     | Within **2 months** of receipt of Rule 45 evidence     | Application is deemed **Abandoned** under Rule 46(2) upon default.                                          |
| **5. Opponent's Evidence in Reply (Rule 47)**    | Rule 47                     | Within **1 month** of receipt of Rule 46 evidence      | Matter proceeds to final Opposition Hearing before the Registrar.                                           |

## How Are Registered Trademarks Enforced Before Commercial Courts in Uttar Pradesh?

Under Section 134(2) of the Trade Marks Act, 1999, a registered trademark proprietor whose principal office is across India can institute a civil suit for trademark infringement directly before the Commercial Court in India, seeking ex-parte ad-interim injunctions, Anton Piller search-and-seizure commissions, and damages.

Unlike ordinary civil suits governed solely by Section 20 of the Code of Civil Procedure (where the plaintiff must sue where the defendant resides or where the cause of action arises), **Section 134(2) of the Trade Marks Act** grants a special jurisdictional privilege (subject to the _Sanjay Dalia_ rule against double overlap): a India-headquartered proprietor can sue an infringer before the **competent Commercial Court**, even if the counterfeit or infringing products are being manufactured outside the district. Additionally, willful counterfeiting attracts criminal search and seizure powers under **Sections 103, 104, and 115 of the Trade Marks Act, 1999**.
