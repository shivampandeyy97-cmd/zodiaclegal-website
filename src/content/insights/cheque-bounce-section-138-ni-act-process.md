Dishonour of cheques remains one of the most frequent commercial defaults encountered by suppliers, MSMEs, non-banking financial companies (NBFCs), and institutional creditors across Uttar Pradesh. To safeguard the credibility of commercial paper, **Chapter XVII (Sections 138 to 148) of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 (NI Act)** establishes a specialized quasi-criminal framework that combines penal deterrence with statutory presumptions and interim monetary recovery.

Because Section 138 creates a technical statutory offence, trial courts—including the designated **Additional Courts (NI Act) and Judicial Magistrates at the District Court Complex in Lucknow**—scrutinize every chronological step with strict exactitude. A single defect in computing notice periods or omitting mandatory corporate averments under Section 141 can result in dismissal at the summoning stage or quashing before the **Allahabad High Court, Lucknow Bench**.

## What Are the Mandatory Statutory Timelines Under Section 138 of the NI Act?

A valid prosecution under Section 138 requires satisfying four strict chronological deadlines: presenting the cheque within 3 months of its date, dispatching a written demand notice within 30 days of receiving the bank return memo, granting the drawer 15 days from notice receipt to pay, and filing the complaint within 1 month after the 15-day period expires.

Under the Proviso to Section 138 read with Section 142(1)(b) of the NI Act, the cause of action does not arise on the date the cheque bounces at the bank; rather, it arises **only on the 16th day after the drawer receives (or is deemed to have received) the statutory demand notice** and fails to make payment.

| Procedural Stage | Governing Provision | Statutory Time Limit | Crucial Compliance Rule |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Presentation of Cheque** | Section 138 Proviso (a) & RBI Guidelines | Within **3 months** from the date written on the cheque | Cheque may be presented any number of times within the 3-month validity window. |
| **2. Dispatch of Demand Notice** | Section 138 Proviso (b) | Within **30 days** of receipt of Bank Return Memo | Notice must demand the **exact cheque amount** (extra interest/damages must be demanded separately). |
| **3. Drawer's Cure Period** | Section 138 Proviso (c) | **15 clear days** from the date of receipt of the demand notice | Filing a complaint *before* the 15-day cure period expires is premature and fatal (*Yogendra Pratap Singh v. Savitri Pandey*). |
| **4. Filing of Court Complaint** | Section 142(1)(b) | Within **1 month** from the date on which the 15-day cure period expires | Delay in filing the complaint (Step 4) can be condoned by the Magistrate upon showing "sufficient cause," unlike delay in Step 2. |

### The "Exact Cheque Amount" Rule in Statutory Notices
A frequent drafting fatal error occurs when a creditor sends a Section 138 notice demanding a consolidated sum (for example, combining a Rs. 15,00,000 cheque amount with Rs. 2,50,000 in overdue invoice interest into an omnibus demand of Rs. 17,50,000 without bifurcation). In ***Suman Sethi v. Ajay K. Churiwal* (2000)**, the Supreme Court held that an omnibus demand that fails to specify the exact dishonoured cheque amount separately renders the statutory notice invalid. Counsel must always state the exact cheque amount distinctly, while listing contractual interest, legal notice fees, or ancillary invoice balances under separate heads.

## How Does Vicarious Liability Work Against Companies and Directors Under Section 141?

When a dishonoured cheque is drawn by a company, LLP, or partnership firm, Section 141 of the NI Act mandates that the company itself must be arrayed as the primary accused, alongside only those directors or partners who were in charge of and responsible to the company for the conduct of its business at the time of the offence.

Following the Constitution Bench judgment of the Supreme Court in ***Aneeta Hada v. Godfather Travels & Tours Pvt. Ltd.* (2012)** and the subsequent principles laid down in ***S.P. Mani and Mohan Dairy v. Dr. Snehalatha Elangovan* (2022)**, prosecuting directors of a defaulting corporate debtor requires strict adherence to three rules:

1. **Company Must Be Accused No. 1:** Without arraigning the juristic entity (the Company or Partnership Firm) as an accused in the complaint, prosecution against its directors or authorized signatories alone is legally unsustainable, except where a statutory moratorium or winding-up order prevents proceeding against the company in its own name (note that under IBC Section 14, while proceedings against the Corporate Debtor are stayed during CIRP, natural person directors remain prosecutable per *P. Mohanraj v. Shah Brothers Ispat Pvt. Ltd.*).
2. **Mandatory Statutory Averments:** Against non-signatory directors, the complaint must explicitly plead that at the time the offence was committed, the accused director was **"in charge of, and was responsible to the company for the conduct of the business of the company."**
3. **Signatory Director vs. Managing Director vs. Independent Director:**
   * **Managing Director / Joint Managing Director:** Liable by virtue of office itself.
   * **Authorized Signatory who signed the cheque:** Directly liable under Section 141(2) without requiring further proof of day-to-day management.
   * **Non-Executive / Independent Directors:** Protected unless specific, unimpeachable factual involvement in the transaction is demonstrated. Before issuing a Section 138 notice to a company registered with ROC Kanpur or elsewhere, diligent practitioners conduct an **MCA V3 Master Data and Signatory Details search** to verify exact DINs, appointment dates, and active director status.

## What Is the Court Procedure Before the Magistrate in Lucknow?

Once the 15-day notice period expires without payment, the payee files a verified complaint under Section 200 CrPC (now Section 223 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023) along with a pre-summoning affidavit (Section 145 NI Act) and original documents before the jurisdictional Magistrate in Lucknow.

Under **Section 142(2) of the NI Act**, territorial jurisdiction is bifurcated based on how the cheque was presented:
* **Collection through an Account (Crossed / Account Payee Cheques):** Jurisdiction vests exclusively with the court within whose local limits the **branch of the bank where the payee maintains the account** is situated. Thus, a Lucknow-based enterprise maintaining its current account at Hazratganj or Gomti Nagar in Lucknow files its Section 138 complaint at the **Lucknow District Courts**, even if the debtor is located in Delhi, Mumbai, or Bengaluru.
* **Presentation Otherwise than through an Account (Bearer Cheques over the counter):** Jurisdiction lies at the branch of the drawee bank where the drawer maintains the account.

### Checklist of Mandatory Original Documents for Filing
* Original dishonoured cheque(s).
* Original Bank Return Memo bearing the official bank stamp/seal or certified digital return advice.
* Office copy of the Statutory Legal Demand Notice sent within 30 days.
* Original Registered Post / Speed Post Postal Receipts and India Post Tracking Report confirming delivery (or returned envelope marked "Refused" / "Unclaimed" / "Door Locked," which constitutes deemed service under Section 27 of the General Clauses Act, 1897).
* Underlying proof of legally enforceable debt: Tax Invoices, Purchase Orders, Ledger Account Statement, Loan Agreement, or Balance Confirmation, supported by a certificate under **Section 63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023** (formerly Section 65B Evidence Act) for electronic records.
* Board Resolution or Power of Attorney authorizing the company representative to institute and prosecute the complaint.

## How Can Creditors Claim 20% Interim Compensation Under Section 143A and Section 148?

Under Section 143A of the NI Act, inserted in 2018, a trial court trying a Section 138 summary or summons case may order the drawer of the cheque to pay interim compensation up to 20% of the cheque amount where the drawer pleads "not guilty." In appeals against conviction, Section 148 mandates a minimum 20% deposit.

To counter dilatory defense tactics, Parliament introduced two powerful liquidity remedies:
1. **Section 143A (Trial Stage Interim Compensation):** Once the accused appears and pleads not guilty to the accusation, the complainant can move an application under Section 143A. While the Supreme Court in ***Rakesh Ranjan Shrivastava v. State of Jharkhand* (2024)** clarified that the power under Section 143A is discretionary rather than mechanical—requiring the Magistrate to evaluate *prima facie* merits, admissions of signature, and financial conduct—a well-documented application backed by undisputed tax invoices or loan ledgers enables recovery of up to **20% of the cheque value within 60 days** (extendable by 30 days), recoverable as a fine under Section 421 CrPC / Section 461 BNSS if unpaid.
2. **Section 148 (Appellate Stage Deposit):** If the trial court convicts the drawer and the drawer appeals to the Sessions Court in Lucknow, the Appellate Court is statutorily empowered under Section 148 to order the appellant to deposit **a minimum of 20% of the fine or compensation awarded** by the trial court as a condition for suspension of sentence.
