## Why Does Precision in Commercial Contract Drafting Matter Under Indian Law?

A commercial contract under the Indian Contract Act, 1872 and the Specific Relief Act, 1963 does more than record a business transaction—it defines payment triggers, allocates operational liabilities, and establishes the procedural roadmap if a relationship breaks down. Relying on generic or foreign-law templates often introduces clauses that conflict with mandatory provisions of Indian statute.

In Uttar Pradesh, businesses frequently encounter disputes not because the underlying commercial intent was unclear, but because the written instrument failed to address scope creep, acceptance testing, defective delivery, tax withholding, or termination procedure. When a dispute reaches the Commercial Court in Lucknow or an arbitral tribunal, adjudicators interpret the plain text of the contract rather than informal assurances exchanged over email or messaging applications.

Furthermore, the 2018 amendments to the Specific Relief Act, 1963 fundamentally shifted Indian contract law by making specific performance a primary remedy under Section 10 and introducing "substituted performance" under Section 20—allowing an aggrieved party to have a broken contract performed by a third party at the defaulting party's cost after issuing a mandatory 30-day written notice. Contracts must therefore be drafted with explicit notice and cure architectures.

## Which Clauses Carry the Highest Financial and Operational Risk?

Whether drafting a Master Services Agreement (MSA), a manufacturing supply contract, an EPC subcontract, or a dealership agreement, five clause clusters determine a party's real legal exposure:

1. **Scope of Work, Acceptance & Payment Milestones:** Ambiguous deliverables allow counterparties to withhold milestone payments indefinitely. Enforceable agreements incorporate objective "deemed acceptance" windows (for example, deliverables are deemed accepted if no written defect notice is issued within 10 business days of delivery).
2. **Indemnity and Limitation of Liability (Sections 124–125, Indian Contract Act):** Under Indian law, an indemnity covers losses caused by the conduct of the promisor or any other person. Well-structured agreements separate direct indemnification obligations (such as third-party IP infringement, statutory non-compliance, or bodily injury) from general breach claims, while imposing a clear aggregate liability cap (typically linked to fees paid in the preceding 6 or 12 months) and excluding indirect or consequential damages.
3. **Liquidated Damages vs. Penalties (Sections 73–74, Indian Contract Act):** Unlike English common law, Section 74 of the Indian Contract Act treats liquidated damages and penalties together, awarding "reasonable compensation not exceeding the amount so named." Drafting must clearly establish why the pre-estimated figure represents a genuine pre-estimate of loss—especially in time-sensitive construction, IT deployment, or supply contracts.
4. **Termination for Cause vs. Termination for Convenience:** Under Section 14(d) of the Specific Relief Act, 1963, contracts that are "in their nature determinable" cannot be specifically enforced. How a termination clause is drafted directly dictates whether a counterparty can obtain a court injunction staying termination.
5. **Restraint of Trade and Exclusivity (Section 27, Indian Contract Act):** Section 27 renders post-termination non-compete clauses largely void outside the sale of business goodwill. Protecting commercial interests therefore requires well-tailored confidentiality obligations, intellectual property assignment provisions, and narrowly defined non-solicitation covenants.

## How Do Stamp Duty and Registration Rules Apply in Uttar Pradesh?

Even a meticulously negotiated agreement can face severe enforcement hurdles if executed on improper stamp paper. Under the Indian Stamp Act, 1899 as amended in its application to Uttar Pradesh (Schedule I-B), every instrument chargeable with duty and executed in Uttar Pradesh must be stamped before or at the time of execution.

Under Section 35 of the Stamp Act, an unstamped or insufficiently stamped instrument cannot be admitted in evidence for any purpose by any person having authority by law or consent of parties to receive evidence (including courts and arbitrators) until the deficit duty and statutory penalty are paid. While the Supreme Court of India has clarified that unstamped arbitration agreements do not prevent the appointment of an arbitrator at the threshold stage, the arbitral tribunal is still statutorily bound to examine and impound insufficiently stamped substantive contracts during proceedings.

Additionally, under Section 17 of the Registration Act, 1908 (as amended in Uttar Pradesh), leases of immovable property from year to year, or for any term exceeding one year, or reserving a yearly rent—as well as agreements to sell immovable property in UP—are compulsorily registrable before the concerned Sub-Registrar. Failure to register such an instrument bars its reception as evidence of any transaction affecting the property under Section 49 of the Registration Act.

## How Should Dispute Resolution and Governing Law Clauses Be Structured?

A defective dispute resolution clause can trap a business in years of procedural litigation before the merits of the claim are ever heard. In commercial contracts involving parties in Lucknow or Uttar Pradesh, the dispute resolution clause should unambiguously specify:

- **Tiered Escalation With Strict Timelines:** If pre-arbitration negotiation or mediation is included, define a strict time limit (e.g., 15 days from written notice) so a defaulting party cannot argue that arbitration is premature.
- **Seat vs. Venue of Arbitration:** Explicitly designate the juridical **seat** of arbitration (e.g., Lucknow, Uttar Pradesh) under Section 20 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, alongside an exclusive jurisdiction clause conferring supervisory authority on the courts at Lucknow.
- **Unilateral Appointment Prohibition:** Following settled Supreme Court jurisprudence (including *Perkins Eastman Architects DPC v. HSCC (India) Ltd.*), a clause granting one party the unilateral right to appoint a sole arbitrator is invalid under Section 12(5) of the Arbitration Act. Clauses must provide for mutual appointment or appointment by an independent arbitral institution or the High Court.
